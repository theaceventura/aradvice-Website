A board receives its monthly cybersecurity report: 99.7% patch compliance, 15 security incidents closed, mean time to detection at 4.2 hours. The chair asks the obvious question: "Are we secure?" The CISO pauses. The metrics don't actually answer what the board needs to know.

This disconnect sits at the heart of cyber governance failure in Australian boardrooms. Directors receive operational performance data when they need strategic risk intelligence. Patch rates tell you about IT hygiene, not business resilience. Incident counts reveal activity, not exposure.

The Cyber Security Act 2024 demands that boards demonstrate "reasonable steps" to protect critical infrastructure. Technical metrics alone cannot substantiate that defence. Directors need frameworks that translate security posture into business terms: which critical processes remain vulnerable, how quickly the organisation can recover from compromise, what level of disruption the business can absorb.

Effective cyber governance starts with asking different questions. Not "how many patches were installed" but "which unpatched systems could halt our operations." Not "how many incidents occurred" but "which attack scenarios could we not detect or contain."

The goal is not perfect security — it is defensible oversight of acceptable risk.
