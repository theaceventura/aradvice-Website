A board chair recently received a pointed question from their largest institutional investor: "Walk me through your board's direct engagement with cyber risk assessment over the past six months. Not what management told you. What you independently verified."

The silence that followed wasn't about classified information. It was about the absence of any meaningful answer.

This is the new reality for 2026 reporting season. Investors are no longer accepting management summaries as evidence of board oversight. They want documentation of independent scrutiny, direct engagement with risk assessment, and defensible proof that directors understand what they're governing.

The Cyber Security Act 2024 provides the statutory framework, but investor expectations have moved beyond compliance. Capital allocation decisions now hinge on whether a board can articulate a convincing governance narrative around digital risk.

Boards that cannot demonstrate independent verification of their cyber risk position will find themselves explaining capital flight, not just regulatory compliance.

The question isn't whether your board has cyber expertise. The question is whether you can prove your governance is more than passive acceptance of technical briefings.
