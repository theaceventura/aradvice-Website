## What the article argues

Outsourcing a business function does not transfer the legal liability that attaches to it, and Australian directors remain personally exposed when a vendor suffers a breach. The article argues that most boards are relying on contracts, indemnity clauses, and vendor self-assessments that provide no real legal protection under the Corporations Act 2001. With ASIC and APRA both escalating scrutiny of supply chain governance, directors need a structured, evidence-based oversight framework rather than a checklist approach.

## Key regulatory and legal context

- **Corporations Act 2001, section 180**: the statutory duty of care and diligence applies to board oversight of third parties and cannot be delegated to a service level agreement or indemnity clause
- **ASIC**: has signalled that oversight of the extended enterprise is a core governance function and continues to focus on whether boards have taken reasonable steps to secure data across the full digital supply chain
- **APRA CPS 230**: raises the bar for operational risk and third-party arrangements, placing full responsibility on boards for service providers that support critical operations
- **Cyber Security Act 2024**: introduced mandatory ransomware payment reporting to government within 72 hours for entities above the relevant turnover threshold, with reporting commencing 30 May 2025
- **SOC 2 reports**: referenced in the article as insufficient on their own because they are point-in-time and limited in scope

## How to respond -- key messages

**On indemnity clauses and personal liability**: Contractual indemnity shifts commercial risk between parties but does not extinguish a director's personal statutory duty under section 180 of the Corporations Act. The question regulators will ask is not what your contract says, but what steps the board actually took to verify the resilience of critical vendors.

**On certifications being sufficient**: A SOC 2 report or ISO certification is a starting point, not a conclusion. Because these are point-in-time assessments with limited scope, relying on them without independent verification is exactly the kind of tick-box approach ASIC is likely to scrutinise after an incident.

**On CPS 230 board obligations**: CPS 230 places the accountability for critical third-party service providers squarely on the board, not on management or procurement. The board needs to understand which vendors support critical operations and have evidence that those vendors are being held to the same standards as internal operations.

**On vendor prioritisation**: The starting point is criticality to essential business functions, not contract value. A low-cost SaaS tool that holds sensitive customer data or sits inside a critical workflow can carry more governance risk than a large facilities contract.

**On ransomware disclosure obligations**: The Cyber Security Act 2024 now requires entities above the turnover threshold to report ransomware payments within 72 hours. Boards need to know whether their vendors are subject to the same obligation and whether their contracts require vendors to notify them promptly of any such incident.

**On what defensible looks like**: Defensible oversight means the board can demonstrate it received meaningful risk metrics, asked substantive questions, and acted on the answers. That requires structured reporting from management focused on verifiable outcomes, not activity logs.

## Advisory angles -- services to position

- **Board-level third-party risk framework review**: assessing whether current vendor oversight processes would withstand regulatory scrutiny after an incident, and helping the board move from questionnaires to independent verification
- **Reporting metrics redesign**: working with management to replace technical activity reports with board-level metrics focused on resilience, criticality, and verifiable control outcomes
- **Vendor criticality mapping workshop**: facilitating a board or audit and risk committee session to categorise vendors by impact on essential business functions and agree oversight tiers
- **CPS 230 readiness review**: for APRA-regulated clients, a structured gap assessment of current third-party governance against CPS 230 expectations
- **Director education session**: a focused briefing for the full board on personal liability exposure from third-party cyber incidents and what regulators expect to see documented
