Your board approved the vendor contract. Your board reviewed the indemnity clause. Your board received a one-page security attestation at the last audit committee meeting. None of that protects you personally when the breach happens at the vendor's end and ASIC asks what oversight you actually exercised.

Contractual indemnity does not transfer fiduciary duty. The Corporations Act 2001 does not recognise "our supplier told us they were secure" as a defence. Yet most boards receive vendor risk reporting that is either a procurement summary or a technical briefing they cannot interrogate, and call that governance.

The structural failure is not the vendor. It is the reporting architecture between management and the board. When your only visibility into a critical SaaS provider is a questionnaire that management completed and management assessed, you have created a governance blind spot that satisfies no one, least of all a regulator reviewing your decisions after a breach.

Defensible oversight requires independent verification of security controls, not attestations. It requires metrics that distinguish inherent risk from residual risk across your critical third parties. It requires a board that can demonstrate, with evidence, that it challenged management's conclusions rather than received them.

The question is not whether your vendors are secure. The question is whether your oversight of them would survive scrutiny. Those are not the same question, and the difference is where director liability lives.
