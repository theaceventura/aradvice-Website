## What the article argues

Andy's core thesis is that AI oversight is now squarely a board-level fiduciary duty under Section 180 of the Corporations Act, not a delegable IT matter. By 2026, directors will no longer be able to claim ignorance of AI-driven failures (biased recruitment tools, flawed financial modelling, privacy breaches); they will need to produce evidence of active, informed oversight. ## How to respond, key messages

- **Personal liability:** Section 180 has always required directors to act on an informed basis; AI doesn't create a new duty, it raises the bar for what "informed" means in practice. The exposure is real but manageable through demonstrable process, not technical mastery.
- **What evidence looks like:** Boards need a documented trail showing they asked the right questions, defined accountabilities, set risk appetite, and sought independent validation of controls. This is a governance artefact (papers, minutes, challenge logs), not a technical audit trail owned by IT.
- **Delegation:** Delegating the operation of AI systems to management remains appropriate; delegating oversight and assurance does not. The board's job is to scrutinise liability while management pursues capability.
- **Relationship to cyber governance:** AI governance should extend existing technology and data governance structures (per AICD guidance) rather than be built as a parallel silo. Boards with mature cyber governance already have most of the scaffolding needed.
- **Committee structure:** No fixed answer in the article; position this as depending on the organisation's existing risk architecture, but the principle is that AI risk appetite and accountability must be explicitly owned, not assumed to be covered by general risk oversight.
- **Regulatory activity:** The article characterises ASIC and ACCC focus as "becoming more focused," which should be presented as a direction of travel rather than a confirmed enforcement wave. Avoid overstating current enforcement activity.
