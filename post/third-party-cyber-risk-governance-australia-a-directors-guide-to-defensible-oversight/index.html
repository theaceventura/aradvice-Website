You can outsource a service, but you cannot outsource the fiduciary risk. A cyber breach at your key supplier is a breach on your watch, and regulators will hold the board accountable for the failure of oversight.

For Australian directors, this accountability is sharpening. The commencement of the Cyber Security Act 2024 on March 4, 2026, alongside persistent scrutiny from ASIC and APRA, means that superficial oversight of supply chain risk is no longer a defensible position. Proving you asked the right questions before a crisis is now a core element of a director’s duty of care and diligence.

The Fiduciary Gap: Third Party Cyber Risk Governance in Australia

Third-party cyber risk governance is not a technical function delegated to the IT department. It is the board’s framework for ensuring that the organisation’s resilience, reputation, and regulatory obligations are not compromised by the actions or failures of its vendors, suppliers, and partners. It is a direct extension of the board’s fiduciary duty.

This creates an ‘Outsource Paradox’. Boards often approve outsourcing to increase efficiency or access specialist skills, assuming risk is transferred along with the function. The opposite is true. Outsourcing a critical function increases the board’s oversight burden because you lose direct control. Your organisation remains accountable for protecting customer data under the Privacy Act 1988, and for maintaining operational resilience under regulatory standards set by APRA. (Third-party risk management (TPRM))

The distinction between management and governance is critical here.

  • Risk Management is the operational task of identifying, assessing, and mitigating threats. This is led by management and technical teams. They select vendors, review security certificates, and monitor performance.
  • Risk Governance is the board’s role. It involves setting the organisation’s risk appetite, demanding a clear line of sight into critical dependencies, and ensuring the systems for managing third-party risk are effective and tested.

Without a strong governance framework, the board is left relying on technical reports that often mask the true nature of supply chain vulnerabilities. This is the fiduciary gap that regulators are now actively targeting.

Regulatory Scrutiny and Director Liability

The standard of care for directors is not static. It evolves with the risk environment. Guidance from the Australian Institute of Company Directors (AICD) makes it clear that digital literacy and a proactive stance on cyber risk are no longer optional. A director cannot claim ignorance of supply chain risks as a defence.

Under the Corporations Act 2001, the duty of care and diligence requires directors to take reasonable steps to protect the company from foreseeable harm. A catastrophic failure at a key cloud provider or data processor is now considered a foreseeable event. The primary shield against regulatory action or shareholder lawsuits is not the absence of a breach, but the presence of a defensible record of oversight. This record must show the board actively interrogated risks, challenged assumptions, and made informed decisions, particularly during the procurement and onboarding of critical vendors.

From the Boardroom

I once sat on a board where our audit and risk committee received quarterly reports on a critical cloud provider. Every report was green. The CISO assured us the vendor had all the right certifications and that our data was secure. The reports were clean, simple, and reassuring.

Six months later, a misconfiguration at that provider exposed our entire customer database. The certifications meant nothing in that moment. The real failure was ours. We, the board, had never asked how the provider’s generic controls mapped to our specific, high-stakes risk appetite. We accepted a technical report as a substitute for governance. We outsourced the service and, in doing so, mistakenly believed we had outsourced the accountability. That incident taught me that a director’s most important job is to ask the questions that connect technical compliance to genuine business resilience.

Establishing a Defensible Framework for Information Technology Procurement

Effective governance of third-party risk begins long before a contract is signed. The procurement process is the board’s first and most powerful lever for establishing defensible oversight. It must be treated as a strategic governance activity, not just a cost-saving exercise.

A governance-ready procurement framework moves beyond price and features to embed resilience from the outset. Its core components must be mandated by the board and understood by management.

  • Risk Tiering: Not all vendors are created equal. The board must insist on a clear methodology for tiering vendors based on their access to sensitive data and criticality to operations. A supplier of office stationery does not require the same scrutiny as a cloud hosting provider or payroll processor.
  • Contractual Mandates: The board should set minimum standards for contracts with critical suppliers. These must include explicit clauses covering security controls, breach notification timelines, and liability. Vague assurances are insufficient.
  • Right-to-Audit Clauses: For the most critical third parties, the contract must grant your organisation the right to audit their security controls. This right provides a crucial verification mechanism beyond relying on the vendor’s self-attestation.

This approach directly addresses the ‘Misalignment Problem’, where a vendor’s standard security posture does not align with your organisation’s specific risk appetite. By ensuring vendor controls are mapped to the board’s stated tolerance for risk during procurement, you build a defensible foundation for the entire relationship.

The Procurement Governance Checklist

The board must challenge management during any significant IT acquisition. The objective is to ensure that governance is built in, not bolted on later. Directors should ask direct, non-technical questions.

  • Who in our organisation owns the risk associated with this new vendor, and how will they report it to the board?
  • What are the specific contractual triggers that require this vendor to notify our board immediately in the event of a breach on their end?
  • How have we verified that this vendor’s security controls are sufficient to protect our most sensitive data, beyond reviewing their own marketing materials?
  • Does this contract give us the ability to conduct our own security assessments or audits if required?

Procurement governance is the first line of defence in supply chain resilience. It provides a documented record of the board’s diligence before an incident occurs.

Moving Beyond the Dashboard: Active Board Oversight

Once a vendor is onboarded, the board’s governance duty shifts to ongoing oversight. Too often, this oversight relies on simplified ‘traffic light’ dashboards presented by the CISO or CIO. These reports, showing a sea of green, can create a dangerous illusion of security.

Technical metrics on their own are meaningless without context. A report stating a vendor has ‘99.9% uptime’ does not tell the board anything about their data breach response plan or their own fourth-party dependencies. Effective oversight requires the board to move beyond passive acceptance of these reports and engage in active interrogation. I have written before about how to challenge a CISO report, and the same principles apply to third-party reporting.

This is where independent, board-level advisory becomes essential. An independent advisor can help the board verify the claims made by internal teams and vendors, translating technical assessments into clear business risk. A structured process like a Cyber Governance Deep Review creates the defensible record that the board was not merely a passive recipient of information but an active, challenging, and diligent governing body.

The Accountability Matrix

Clear accountability is the cornerstone of good governance. The board must define and document who is responsible for third-party risk. This often involves assigning primary oversight to a specific committee, such as the Audit, Risk, or a dedicated Technology committee. This committee is then responsible for:

  • Reviewing the risk tiering of all critical vendors at least annually.
  • Receiving and interrogating management’s reports on vendor performance and security posture.
  • Translating complex vendor reports, such as SOC 2 attestations, into strategic risk insights for the full board. A clean SOC 2 report is not a guarantee of security; the board needs to understand what the report’s scope covers and, more importantly, what it excludes.

Testing Resilience Through Simulation

The ultimate test of any plan is how it performs under pressure. The board should mandate that key third-party vendors are included in the organisation’s incident response simulations. A theoretical plan for how a cloud provider will notify you of a breach is one thing; testing that communication path in a simulated crisis is another.

These simulations reveal the true strength of your contractual agreements and communication protocols. They provide invaluable, real-world evidence of your organisation’s collective resilience. The insights gained from these exercises form a critical part of the ongoing cyber risk reporting to the board, demonstrating a mature and proactive governance posture.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between third-party risk management and third-party risk governance?
Risk management is the operational process of identifying and mitigating risks, handled by management. Risk governance is the board’s responsibility to set the risk appetite and ensure the management systems are effective, providing strategic oversight and accountability.

Can a board be held legally liable for a cyber breach that occurred at a third-party vendor?
Yes. Under the Corporations Act 2001, directors have a duty of care and diligence. If a foreseeable risk from a third party materialises and the board cannot demonstrate it took reasonable steps to oversee that risk, directors may be found to have breached their duties.

How often should an Australian board receive reports on third-party cyber risk?
For critical vendors, the relevant board committee should receive detailed reports at least quarterly. The full board should receive a strategic summary of key third-party risks as a standing agenda item, also at least quarterly. The frequency should increase if the risk profile changes.

What are the most critical 'Director’s Questions' to ask about IT procurement?
The most critical questions focus on accountability and verification. Ask: “Who owns this risk?” “How will we be notified in a crisis?” and “How have we independently verified this vendor’s security claims beyond their own reports?”
