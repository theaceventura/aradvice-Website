Consider a chair asked by their insurer to demonstrate what oversight the board actually exercised before a breach occurred. Not what policies were approved. Not what management reported. What the board itself did to test, challenge, and verify. Most chairs cannot answer that question with evidence.

Section 180 of the Corporations Act has always required care and diligence. The full enforcement of the Cyber Security Act 2024 converts that general standard into a specific accountability framework, and the question shifts from whether your board received cyber briefings to whether those briefings were sufficient to discharge a legal duty of oversight.

The distinction matters because the two things are not the same. Receiving a technical dashboard is not oversight. Approving a policy is not oversight. Defensible oversight leaves a documented record of directed inquiry, substantive challenge, and evidence-based assurance that goes beyond what management chose to present.

ASIC has been explicit about the evidentiary standard it expects. Directors who cannot point to that record are exposed, regardless of whether a breach occurs, because the duty attaches to the process of governance, not to the outcome.

The practical implication is this: if your board cannot reconstruct its own oversight record from board papers alone, the oversight did not happen in any legally meaningful sense.
