## What the article argues

Australian directors can no longer treat cyber risk as a technical matter safely delegated to management, because the Cyber Security Act 2024 creates a direct link between oversight quality and personal liability. Green dashboard reporting that measures tool existence rather than effectiveness is not a defensible governance position. With enforcement appetite now demonstrated through the courts, boards must shift from passive recipients of technical metrics to active governors who demand independently verified, financially framed risk reporting.

## Key regulatory and legal context

- **Cyber Security Act 2024**: The central piece of legislation creating director-level obligations around cyber oversight; full enforcement commences 1 January 2026
- **Section 180 of the Corporations Act 2001**: The statutory duty of care and diligence that frames cyber risk as a core fiduciary obligation, not an IT function
- **ASIC v FIIG Securities Limited [2026] FCA 92**: Federal Court penalty of A$2.5 million imposed on FIIG Securities for inadequate cyber risk management; the first such penalty against an Australian financial services licensee; penalty fell on the company, not directors personally
- **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC)**: Referenced as the source of technical benchmarks that boards are expected to apply strategic weight to, not simply receive passively
- **ASIC**: Implied as the enforcement body through the FIIG Securities action against a financial services licensee

## How to respond -- key messages

**On green dashboards and quarterly CISO reporting**: Operational metrics like patch rates and uptime measure activity, not exposure. The question to ask management is whether any of that reporting translates technical vulnerability into financial impact or legal consequence. If it does not, the board has a reporting gap, not a governance program.

**On the FIIG penalty falling on the company not directors**: The FIIG outcome reflects the enforcement pathway available today under licensee obligations, but it signals where ASIC is prepared to go. The Cyber Security Act 2024 creates a separate and more direct set of obligations that shift the frame toward individual director conduct. The absence of personal liability in FIIG is not a ceiling.

**On what independent verification looks like**: Independent verification means periodic engagement of someone outside the internal team to assess whether controls are configured and effective, not merely deployed. It does not have to be continuous, but it needs to be documentable and evidenced in board minutes.

**On whether their current process protects them**: A governance process protects you when you can demonstrate you asked the right questions, received independently substantiated answers, and acted on what you found. A paper trail of accepted assurances, without challenge or verification, is unlikely to satisfy the standard of care a regulator or court would apply.

## Watch-outs

- **The personal liability framing needs care**: The article argues compellingly that director exposure is real, but the FIIG case it cites imposed a penalty on the company, not individual directors. Andy should be precise about the distinction between current enforcement practice and the future risk trajectory rather than suggesting personal penalties are an immediate certainty.
- **The Cyber Security Act 2024 obligations are still being tested**: The article describes the regulatory expectation with confidence, but the specific standard of conduct that would satisfy or breach director obligations under the Act has not yet been definitively settled by a court or regulator. Andy should position advice around defensibility and reasonable precaution rather than implying there is a fixed compliance checklist.
- **Not all clients are financial services licensees**: The FIIG enforcement action arose under financial services licensing obligations specific to that sector. A sophisticated client from outside financial services may correctly note that their regulatory baseline differs, and Andy should be ready to address sector-specific variation rather than treating FIIG as universally applicable.
