## What the article argues

Australian directors can no longer treat cyber risk as a technical matter safely delegated to management, because the Cyber Security Act 2024 creates a direct link between oversight quality and personal liability. Accepting green-dashboard reporting without independent verification of what those metrics actually measure is now a governance failure, not a defensible position. The enforcement appetite signalled by recent Federal Court action means the regulatory risk is real and immediate.

## Key regulatory and legal context

- **Cyber Security Act 2024**: The central legislative framework discussed; creates obligations that attach directly to directors, not just organisations
- **Corporations Act 2001, Section 180**: The duty of care and diligence provision that frames cyber risk as a core fiduciary obligation, not a delegable technical function
- **ASIC v FIIG Securities Limited [2026] FCA 92**: Federal Court imposed a A$2.5 million penalty on FIIG Securities for inadequate cyber risk management; the first such penalty against an Australian financial services licensee; the penalty fell on the company under licensee obligations, not on directors personally
- **ASIC**: Identified as the enforcing regulator in the FIIG matter; signals enforcement appetite boards must govern against
- **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC)**: Cited as the source of technical benchmarks that inform but do not substitute for board-level strategic oversight

## How to respond, key messages

**On personal versus company liability:** The FIIG penalty landed on the company, but the article is clear that director liability under Section 180 of the Corporations Act runs separately and in parallel. A company penalty does not exhaust the regulator's options, and a director who passively accepted management assurances has a weaker defence than one who actively challenged and verified them.

**On the adequacy of quarterly CISO reporting:** The article's central point is that operational metrics such as patch rates and uptime measure the existence of controls, not their effectiveness or configuration. Andy's position is that a board receiving only this type of reporting is not exercising fiduciary oversight, regardless of how polished the slides are.

**On independent verification:** Independent verification means someone other than the management team responsible for cyber operations reviews whether controls are actually working as reported. Andy should be direct that this is a governance structure question, not just a technical audit question, and that the board needs to own the commissioning of that verification.

**On what a defensible position looks like:** A defensible position requires documented evidence that the board asked the right questions, challenged the technical narrative, sought verification beyond management assertion, and connected technical findings to financial and legal exposure. A folder of green dashboards is not that evidence.

## Watch-outs

- **The FIIG case should not be overstated**: The penalty in that matter fell on the company under its licensee obligations, not on individual directors. Andy should be careful not to imply that directors face direct personal fines under the same facts without being precise about the distinct liability pathways involved.
- **The article does not fully resolve what "reasonable steps" looks like in practice**: The gap between what the Cyber Security Act 2024 requires and what a regulator would actually accept as a defensible board process is still being worked out. A sophisticated client may push back by asking for precise standards, and Andy should be honest that regulatory guidance on director-specific obligations is still developing.
- **Not all clients are in the same position**: The article is written for a general director audience, but the obligations and risk profile differ materially depending on whether the client is a critical infrastructure operator, a financial services licensee, or a general ASX-listed company. Andy should resist applying the same urgency framing uniformly without first understanding the client's specific regulatory context.
